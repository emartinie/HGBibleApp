Two Powers
in Heaven?

The God of Israel is one. Yet Scripture describes divine visitors, a messenger bearing God’s name, and a heavenly figure receiving everlasting rule. How do these passages illuminate the Father and Son?

When YHWH appears to Abraham, and later brings judgment “from YHWH” in heaven, what distinction is the text asking us to notice?

How to read this investigation: Begin with the text, compare explanations, and follow the sources. Labels distinguish textual observations, interpretations, and debated historical conclusions. You do not need to agree with one theological system to examine the evidence.
The question in brief

One God. Distinction worth examining.

“Two powers in heaven” is a historical expression associated with controversies over heavenly authority. Here we investigate whether biblical descriptions of God and a distinguishable divine figure help explain the New Testament’s Father-and-Son language.

Textual observation

The pattern is present

Some passages move between messenger language and God’s own identity, presence, or speech. Others distinguish an enthroned God from a second figure.

Interpretive proposal

Father and Son

A Christian reading identifies certain divine appearances with the preexistent Son, revealing the unseen Father. That identification requires comparing passages.

Historical question

How widely understood?

Ancient Jewish and Christian sources preserve different explanations. Their existence does not establish a single doctrine accepted by everyone.

Deuteronomy 6:4 and Mark 12:29 anchor the discussion in the oneness of Israel’s God. The investigation concerns how that God is revealed and represented.

Read Genesis in order

1. Is Abraham’s hospitality the first example?

It depends on what qualifies as an example. Plural language such as Genesis 1:26 raises questions but does not explicitly identify Father and Son. God’s earlier appearances likewise do not, by themselves, distinguish two divine figures.

Hagar’s encounter in Genesis 16:7–13 is an earlier, more concrete candidate. The speaker is introduced as the angel of YHWH; the narrator subsequently identifies the one speaking to her as YHWH, whom Hagar addresses as the God who sees her. This juxtaposition belongs in the investigation. Read Genesis 16:7–13 ↗

Working distinction: Genesis 16 supplies an early messenger/God identification to examine. Genesis 18–19 develops the question through a longer narrative, culminating in YHWH acting “from YHWH” in heaven. Neither passage names its visitor Yeshua.

“First in the Bible’s narrative order” also differs from “earliest composed text.” This investigation follows narrative order; it does not establish the historical order of composition.

The central narrative

2. Abraham’s visitors: follow the whole scene

Read Genesis 18–19 together. The argument rests on how the story identifies its speakers and visitors, rather than simply on the number three.

  • Genesis 18:1–2: YHWH appears to Abraham, who sees three men.
  • Genesis 18:8–15: The visitors receive hospitality; plural speech gives way to an individual speaker, and YHWH is identified as speaking.
  • Genesis 18:22–33: Men turn toward Sodom while Abraham speaks with YHWH.
  • Genesis 19:1: Two angels arrive at Sodom.
  • Genesis 19:24: YHWH rains judgment from YHWH out of heaven.
וַיהוָה … מֵאֵת יְהוָה מִן־הַשָּׁמָיִםYHWH … from YHWH, from heaven.
Genesis 19:24, abbreviated Hebrew wording with a literal gloss.

The repeated divine name is explicit. Reading it as two distinguishable divine figures is an interpretation of that wording within the narrative. A repeated name alone does not settle the question.

Does three visitors mean three divine persons?

The passage does not call the visitors Father, Son, and Spirit. The particular Father-and-Son argument examined here reads one visitor as divine and two as angels, while the Father remains in heaven. That must be evaluated against alternative readings.

Test the explanations

3. What else could the narrative mean?

A divine visitor and two angels

This reading connects the appearance, YHWH’s speech, the two angels reaching Sodom, and the heaven/earth distinction. Christian interpreters may identify the visitor as the Son.

Its task: establish why the visitor should be identified as the Son rather than leaving the appearance unspecified.

Three angels, with God distinguished

Rashi’s commentary assigns missions to the three visitors and explains why only two continue to Sodom. God’s appearance and Abraham’s conversation with God can be distinguished from the angelic visit.

Its task: account for the narrative’s transitions between visitors and divine speech.

Authorized divine agency

A messenger may communicate the sender’s words and authority. Some readers understand the close identification in these passages through representation.

Its task: determine whether representation explains every feature, or whether particular texts imply a stronger identification.

Compare Rashi on Genesis 18:2–3 and 33 ↗

Ancient witnesses

4. Ancient readers noticed these questions too

Philo: a first-century Jewish reading of Abraham’s visitors

In On Abraham 119–125, Philo interprets the visitors allegorically through God and God’s creative and royal powers. This is direct evidence of a first-century Jewish exploration of divine manifestations using Genesis 18.

His two powers alongside God are not identical to the later rabbinic controversy or to Father-and-Son theology. The shared vocabulary must not erase the differences.

Read Philo, On Abraham, 119–125 ↗
Justin Martyr: the visitor identified as the preexistent Christ

In the second-century Dialogue with Trypho, chapters 56–60, Justin argues that Abraham encountered a divine figure distinct from the Father, accompanied by two angels. He identifies that figure with the preexistent Christ and uses Genesis 19:24 in the discussion.

This establishes an early Christian precedent for the interpretation. Justin’s literary dialogue is evidence for his argument; it is not an independently verified transcript of Jewish agreement. His hostile rhetoric toward Jews should not become our teaching voice.

Read Dialogue with Trypho, chapters 56–60 ↗
Sanhedrin 38b: the very wording of Genesis 19:24 is disputed

An interlocutor challenges a rabbi over the repeated divine name in Genesis 19:24. The answer compares the repetition of Lamech’s name in Genesis 4:23: repeating a name need not imply two individuals.

The same talmudic page also discusses a name-bearing angel and the thrones in Daniel 7. These are distinct exchanges to examine before combining them into a larger argument.

Read Sanhedrin 38b ↗
Chagigah 15a: Metatron and two heavenly authorities

Elisha ben Abuyah sees Metatron seated and concludes that there may be two authorities. The story rejects his conclusion. It preserves a dispute about an exalted heavenly figure, not rabbinic endorsement of a second deity.

The Babylonian Talmud was compiled centuries after the first century, while preserving earlier traditions. Named speakers and narrative settings do not, by themselves, date every detail. Later Metatron traditions cannot simply be assigned to the Second Temple period.

Read Chagigah 15a ↗
Historical finding: Divine manifestations and heavenly intermediaries were subjects of Jewish interpretation. The surviving sources preserve disagreement about their meaning and limits. “Two powers” is not a single ancient creed that all these writers shared.
Compare the passages

5. Does the pattern continue?

The following texts extend the investigation. Each needs its own context; their inclusion does not mean every passage proves the same conclusion.

God and the messenger: Exodus, Jacob, and Judges
  • Exodus 3:2–6: The angel appears in the bush; God speaks from it. How does the narrative relate the messenger to God?
  • Exodus 23:20–23: God sends an angel and says His name is in him. What does bearing the divine name entail?
  • Genesis 48:15–16: Jacob invokes God and the redeeming angel in blessing Joseph’s sons.
  • Judges 6:11–24; 13:15–22: Messenger language and encounters understood as seeing God occur together.
Open these passages ↗
The heavenly court: Psalm 110 and Daniel 7

Psalm 110:1 distinguishes YHWH from the lord addressed. The Hebrew titles differ: the verse does not use YHWH twice.

Daniel 7:9–14 depicts the Ancient of Days and a humanlike figure receiving dominion. Verses 18 and 27 also describe the holy ones receiving the kingdom. Individual, representative, and collective interpretations must be tested against the complete chapter.

Sanhedrin 38b preserves an interpretation attributed to Rabbi Akiva assigning a throne to God and one to David, alongside objections and other explanations. A recorded disagreement does not establish consensus.

Read Psalm 110:1 and Daniel 7 ↗
The Father and Son: John, Mark, and Paul
  • John 1:1–18: The Word is with God, is identified as God, becomes flesh, and makes the unseen God known.
  • John 8:56–58: Yeshua speaks of Abraham and His own existence before Abraham. The passage does not explicitly identify Genesis 18’s visitor.
  • John 17:1–5: The Son addresses the Father and speaks of shared glory before the world existed.
  • Mark 14:61–64: Right-hand enthronement and coming with the clouds are combined in Yeshua’s answer.
  • 1 Corinthians 8:4–6: Paul affirms one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ.

What follows—and what remains open

6. A Father-and-Son reading deserves examination

Read together, these passages support investigating whether visible divine appearances anticipate the Son who makes the Father known. This interpretation has ancient Christian precedent and meaningful points of contact with Jewish reflection on divine agency and heavenly figures.

Historical connections do not establish that Philo, the rabbis, and the New Testament authors taught one identical system. Nor does identifying two distinguishable figures settle every question about their nature or relationship.

Does this settle the question of the Spirit?

No. Some readers understand the Spirit as shared divine presence and power; Trinitarian readers understand the Spirit as a distinct divine person. The texts about the Spirit require their own investigation. The historical phrase “two powers” cannot decide that issue by itself.

Likewise, traditions about two messiahs concern another question. Two messiahs, two heavenly authorities, and Father-and-Son language must not be treated as interchangeable categories.

First-pass conclusion: Hagar’s encounter is an earlier concrete candidate for the messenger/God pattern. Abraham’s hospitality and Genesis 19:24 form an especially significant sequence. A claim that this is the first explicit revelation of Father and Son requires a further argument: the passage itself does not supply those names.

7. Questions to carry into conversation

  1. Which identifications are made by the narrator, and which do we supply?
  2. What would help distinguish an authorized representative from a divine manifestation?
  3. What does each explanation account for well? What remains difficult?
  4. Which New Testament connections are explicit quotations, and which are interpretive links?
  5. What evidence could change our preferred interpretation?
